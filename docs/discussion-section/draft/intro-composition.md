# Intro: new paragraph on automated composition (draft)

Automatically composing workflows from annotated tools has been attempted before. Early efforts treated composition as AI planning over semantic web services (McIlraith & Son 2002), and bioinformatics registries such as BioMoby and SADI annotated services with ontologies of data types so that chains of services could be discovered from a query (Wilkinson & Links 2002; Wilkinson et al. 2011). Pegasus and Wings applied knowledge-based planning to generate grid workflows (Blythe et al. 2003 [verify]; Gil et al. 2011 [verify]), and PROPHETS and APE synthesise candidate workflows from tools annotated with the EDAM ontology (Lamprecht et al. 2010 [verify]; Kasalica & Lamprecht 2020). These systems produce workflows at design time, but adoption has been limited by incomplete tool annotations, the difficulty of ranking alternative workflows, and the gap between a synthesised workflow and its execution at scale (Lamprecht et al. 2021). Meanwhile, tree search has made planning over large combinatorial spaces tractable. Monte Carlo tree search guided by neural networks mastered Go and chess (Silver et al. 2016; Silver et al. 2018), and the same approach planned chemical syntheses by chaining reaction rules backwards from a target molecule to purchasable precursors (Segler et al. 2018). Workflow composition has the same shape: tools are rules, and a workflow is a route from available data to a requested product.

What I need from you

1. The raw reads, as FASTQ.

Your Globus folder has the assemblies Plasmidsaurus built, their annotation and their QC plots, which is a good delivery, but not the reads themselves. I would rather assemble from the reads than take someone else's assembly on trust: an insertion sits at a join between your cargo and the genome, and a join is exactly where an assembler can go wrong. Starting from reads means I can check the coverage across each junction instead of assuming it.

Your order page should have a reads download. Dropping it into the same folder is easiest.

2. The modified-base file, if Plasmidsaurus will give you one.

Methylation is not readable from an assembly or from plain FASTQ. It needs the file the basecaller writes with per-read modified-base tags, usually a BAM. If your order includes methylation, that file exists; if it does not, the methylation part of this cannot run and the rest still can.

What the pipeline does

It runs per colony, and every colony meets the same reference and the same copy of your construct, so the three results are comparable.

  1. Read quality, then trimming. Your reads are nanopore at a median quality around Q23, which is good enough that the assembler uses its high-accuracy setting rather than its raw-nanopore one.
  2. Assemble each colony with flye. Plasmidsaurus got one closed circular contig per colony, so I expect the same.
  3. Call and annotate the genes with prodigal, then UniRef50 and KOfam, on each colony's own assembly.
  4. Fetch the published EPI300 genome (__ACC__) to compare against.
  5. Find your cargo and say where it sits.
  6. List everything else that differs from EPI300.
  7. Compare the colonies to each other.
  8. Methylation, if the modified-base file arrives.

How I tell a clean insertion from a messy one

Rather than searching for your plasmid as one block, I cut it into its annotated features first and look for each one separately. That distinction matters: if the whole plasmid is present, including the delivery backbone and its origin of replication, then it is sitting in the cell as a plasmid rather than having gone into the landing pad. A clean insertion looks like the cargo between the two recombinase sites and nothing from the backbone.

Then I take the colony sequence just outside each end of the cargo and look up where it falls in EPI300. That is what names the insertion site, and it tells you whether both junctions landed where the landing pad is meant to be. One junction in the right place and one somewhere else means something other than the intended recombination happened.

You also get a per-feature identity table, so if part of the cargo is truncated or carries a mutation, it is named rather than hidden inside an overall percentage.

Everything else that changed

Each colony is aligned to EPI300 and the differences are listed with the genes they affect: single-base changes, small insertions and deletions, and anything larger. The cargo itself comes back as an insertion, so it is labelled as the insertion rather than counted as damage.

The colonies are also aligned to each other. Colonies picked off one plate after one transformation are often siblings rather than independent events, and that changes how you read a difference found in only one of them: in a sibling it is probably something that happened during growth, not three independent confirmations of the same thing.

Methylation

Your construct carries four methyltransferases, and its annotation names what each one targets:

CTCGAGCTGCAGGGCCATTAAT

So the useful test is not whether the genome is methylated but whether it is methylated at those motifs and not elsewhere. That is what separates enzymes that are present from enzymes that are working, and it is measured against the colony's own background rather than against an expectation.
